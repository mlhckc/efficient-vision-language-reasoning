\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Motivation and problem}
\label{sec:ch1-motivation}

Visual question answering (VQA) asks a system to combine information from an
image with a natural-language question in order to produce an answer. It
therefore provides a focused setting for studying how visual and linguistic
representations interact, rather than assessing either modality in isolation.
Closed-answer classification has been part of VQA since its early benchmarks
\citep{antol2015vqa}. GQA adds questions derived from scene graphs and
associated functional programs, making it possible to examine both overall
answer accuracy and performance profiles over questions with different
program lengths \citep{hudson2019gqa}.

\begingroup
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{1em}
Vision--language systems combine pretrained visual and linguistic components
in several ways. Frozen language-model interfaces, latent resamplers and
query-based connectors provide important architectural precedents
\citep{tsimpoukelli2021frozen,alayrac2022flamingo,li2023blip2,jaegle2021perceiver}.
A complementary line of work shows that frozen embeddings can also support VQA
through comparatively restricted trainable classifiers or bridges
\citep{deuser2022less,merullo2023limber}. These approaches
motivate a controlled question: how much of the task can be handled by frozen
representations and compact trainable interfaces, and what changes when the
interface receives richer token access or a frozen small language model?\par
\endgroup

This dissertation uses closed-set GQA to study that question while separating
representation choice, explicit multimodal interaction, lightweight latent
reasoning and language-model pretraining. Compactness is not treated as proof
of speed. Runtime efficiency is evaluated through valid end-to-end warm-serial
latency, while parameter counts provide complementary capacity and system-size
context. Neither trainable nor total loaded parameter counts are treated as
measurements of latency, storage, energy or monetary cost.

\section{Research gap and aim}
\label{sec:ch1-gap}

The literature provides global-embedding heads, learned latent connectors and
minimal interfaces to frozen language models, but each design answers a
different architectural question under its own task and protocol. This
dissertation therefore studies representation quality, explicit multimodal
interaction, lightweight latent reasoning and frozen language-model
pretraining within one controlled experimental framework. Matched controls,
nested training scales and a common development discipline are used to keep
the comparisons interpretable without turning cross-family observations into
causal claims.

The aim is to evaluate, under a controlled development protocol, whether
efficient closed-set visual question answering can be achieved with frozen
representations, compact trainable heads and small-language-model interfaces,
and to characterise where accuracy gains and measured runtime costs arise.
This aim concerns the implemented GQA setting and measured quantities; it is
not a claim about general multimodal intelligence or deployment suitability.

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:ch1-rqs}

The dissertation is organised around three research questions.

\phantomsection
\label{rq:rq1}
\noindent\textbf{RQ1.} To what extent can lightweight heads built on frozen global image and question embeddings answer closed-set GQA questions, and how do explicit interaction features, frozen-encoder choice and answer-set size affect development-set accuracy?

Chapter 4 answers RQ1 through global-head baselines, interaction and capacity
controls, visual-reliance interventions, an alternative frozen encoder and a
larger closed answer space.

\phantomsection
\label{rq:rq2}
\noindent\textbf{RQ2.} As training data scales, does a lightweight 32-query latent reasoner over frozen token-level visual features improve on global-embedding heads, and do any overall gains extend to questions requiring four or more reasoning steps?

Chapter 5 answers RQ2 through scale comparisons, token-input diagnostics and
a fixed-prior compositional profile based on GQA program length.

\phantomsection
\label{rq:rq3}
\noindent\textbf{RQ3.} When a frozen small language model is coupled to the latent reasoner on the question side or used as the answer readout, does pretraining improve development-set accuracy over an architecturally matched random control, and what efficiency implications are supported by the project's trainable-parameter counts and valid warm-serial latency measurements?

Chapter 6 answers the pretraining and placement part of RQ3, and Chapter 7
establishes the bounded efficiency implications under the governed runtime
protocol.

\section{Approach and contributions}
\label{sec:ch1-approach}

The experiments use an image-disjoint GQA development partition, nested
training scales and closed answer vocabularies derived from the training pool.
The top-100 and top-1000 vocabularies are treated as different supported-answer
views rather than as one interchangeable metric. The global pathway combines
frozen CLIP image and question representations \citep{radford2021clip} with compact heads, including
explicit product and difference interactions and capacity controls.
An alternative frozen SigLIP representation \citep{zhai2023siglip} tests an encoder change under its
implemented interface.

The token pathway uses a 32-query latent reasoner over frozen visual and
question features. Frozen SmolLM2 representations \citep{benallal2025smollm2} are then introduced on the
question side, or the frozen model is used after the reasoner as an answer
readout; pretrained variants are compared with architecture-matched random
controls.
These are the project's core small-language-model interfaces. Evaluation uses
predefined seeds and development-only selection rules, with each contrast
interpreted according to its matching and selection class. Runtime is assessed
separately through governed end-to-end warm-serial measurement, rather than
being inferred from architecture names or parameter counts. The experimental
conclusions reported in this dissertation are based on the governed
development evidence; held-out evaluation remains pending.

This dissertation contributes a controlled evaluation of compact
frozen-representation VQA heads with interaction and capacity controls. It
contributes an evaluation of the 32-query latent reasoner across training
scales, including token-input and compositional-profile diagnostics. It also
contributes matched pretrained-versus-random controls for question- and
answer-side small-language-model interfaces, including a larger answer-side
extension, and a governed end-to-end warm-serial efficiency evaluation with a
common-denominator accuracy--latency analysis. A supporting reproducibility
contribution is the explicit provenance and status tracking of reported
measurements, including the separation of retained, superseded and withdrawn
evidence.

The qualitative development results give a bounded answer to the title
question. Compact frozen-representation systems achieve useful accuracy on the
governed closed-set task, and representation and interaction choices
materially affect the observed results. The latent reasoner improves overall
configuration-level accuracy with training scale without eliminating the
observed pooled multi-step deficit. On the question side, SmolLM2 pretraining
has a positive advantage relative to its matched random control; on the answer
side, no reliable positive pretraining advantage is detected under the tested
conditions. The canonical measured accuracy--latency frontier contains the
non-SLM concatenation, fusion and top-1000 product systems. An SLM is therefore
not required for the strongest measured trade-offs in this study, and its
observed contribution remains interface-specific rather than universal.

\section{Dissertation structure}
\label{sec:ch1-structure}

Chapter 2 positions the study within relevant research on VQA, frozen
representations and latent interfaces, while Chapter 3 specifies the data
partitions, scoring rules, architectures and evaluation protocol. Chapters 4,
5 and 6 answer RQ1,
RQ2 and RQ3 through the global heads, latent reasoner and small-language-model
interfaces, respectively; Chapter 7 then reports the governed efficiency
analysis. Chapter 8 interprets the results and their limitations across the
research questions, and Chapter 9 states the development-bounded conclusions,
contributions and future work.
